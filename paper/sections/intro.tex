Guided-wave structural health monitoring (SHM) relies on repeated waveform
measurements, yet environmental variation can change the signal relative to a
baseline. Temperature compensation is a documented consideration in
guided-wave SHM \cite{croxford2010}. At the same time, storing or transmitting
every compensated waveform can be costly. These facts motivate a narrow
representation question: after compensation, can a sparse event stream retain
the information needed for a record-level comparison without being given an
accounting or selection advantage?

Send-on-delta (SoD) is an established event-based reporting strategy
\cite{miskowicz2006}. It emits changes rather than a uniformly sampled
sequence, which makes it a plausible representation for a compensated
residual. That plausibility alone does not establish a codec advantage.
Earlier project diagnostics compared SoD with a matched-count
zero-order-hold baseline, selected thresholds from small sweeps, and used a
long-term month without a disjoint alarm-calibration period. Those choices
leave four review-critical questions unresolved: whether timestamps and
packet framing are charged, whether model side information is visible,
whether test labels influence selection, and whether a reported alert is
newly triggered after a future transition.

This paper asks three connected but non-pooled questions. First, under a fixed
per-record byte capacity, how does bounded SoD compare with uniform linear
interpolation, PCA, and Haar discrete wavelet coding on held-out guided-wave
records? Second, when a cold-start alarm is calibrated only on a healthy
month, what false-call, newly started alarm-delay, and coverage outcomes
appear on the following month? Third, which applicability statements do those
two distinct audits allow, and which generalization, deployment, and causal
claims do they exclude? The questions do not ask whether SoD is a universal
replacement for dense acquisition or whether one observed onset estimates a
population probability of detection.

Answering them requires protocol rather than model complexity. The unit of
analysis must be a monitoring record, not individual paths from the same
record. Codec configurations must be selected without damage labels or test
records, and packet sizes must be measured after serialization. The alarm
replay must fit its reference bank, normalization, event delta, and threshold
grid before the future stream is scored. Finally, a persistent or pre-onset
alert must not be counted as a new detection.

We address these requirements with a frozen strict evaluation and an explicit
evidence-eligibility rule. The codec experiment uses date-separated healthy
training, validation, and test periods, a training-only signed-16-bit
residual contract, and four predeclared hard capacities. It serializes bounded
SoD packets as well as uniform, PCA, and Haar packets and reports decoder
model bytes separately. The alarm experiment calibrates on March 2021, scores
all of April 2021 before labels are read for reporting, and evaluates every
one of nine March-derived thresholds for both dense residual energy and
Level-A SoD event count. The two completed audits are reported as separate
scope-bounded results; historical exploratory and invalidated mechanism-chain
artifacts do not enter selection, tables, figures, or conclusions.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it specifies a reproducible
evaluation contract that prevents path pseudoreplication, uncharged payload,
test-set operating-point selection, and label leakage. Second, it reports
the complete all-capacity codec result: bounded SoD does not lead any tested
capacity for either D04 or D24. Third, it reports the complete cold-start
alarm grid without selecting a favourable threshold and states the resulting
applicability boundary: the observed false-call, delay, and coverage outcomes
do not support an operational-alarm claim. The OGW resource and the
long-term guided-wave dataset are documented by Moll et al.\ \cite{moll2019ogw}
and Yang et al.\ \cite{yang2025dataset}, respectively.
