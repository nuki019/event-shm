Guided-wave structural health monitoring (SHM) relies on repeated waveform
measurements, yet environmental variation can change the signal relative to a
baseline. Temperature compensation is therefore an important consideration in
many guided-wave monitoring workflows \cite{croxford2010}. At the same time,
storing or transmitting every compensated waveform can be costly. These two
facts motivate a narrow representation question: after compensation, can a
sparse event stream retain the information needed for a record-level
comparison without being given an accounting or selection advantage?

Send-on-delta (SoD) is an established event-based reporting strategy
\cite{miskowicz2006}. It emits changes rather than a uniformly sampled
sequence, which makes it a plausible representation for a compensated
residual. That plausibility alone does not establish a codec advantage.
Earlier project diagnostics compared SoD with a matched-count
zero-order-hold baseline, selected thresholds from small sweeps, and used a
long-term month without a disjoint alarm-calibration period. Those choices
leave four review-critical questions unresolved: whether timestamps and
packet framing are charged, whether model side information is visible,
whether test labels influence selection, and whether a reported alert is
newly triggered after a future transition.

This paper asks two testable questions. First, under a fixed per-record byte
capacity, how does bounded SoD compare with uniform linear interpolation,
PCA, and Haar discrete wavelet coding on held-out guided-wave records?
Second, when a cold-start alarm is calibrated only on a healthy month, what
false-call, newly started alarm-delay, and coverage outcomes appear on the
following month? These questions do not ask whether SoD is a universal
replacement for dense acquisition or whether one observed onset estimates a
population probability of detection.

Answering them requires protocol rather than model complexity. The unit of
analysis must be a monitoring record, not individual paths from the same
record. Codec configurations must be selected without damage labels or test
records, and packet sizes must be measured after serialization. The alarm
replay must fit its reference bank, normalization, event delta, and threshold
grid before the future stream is scored. Finally, a persistent or pre-onset
alert must not be counted as a new detection.

We address these requirements with a frozen strict evaluation. The codec
experiment uses date-separated healthy training, validation, and test
periods, a training-only signed-16-bit residual contract, and four
predeclared hard capacities. It serializes bounded SoD packets as well as
uniform, PCA, and Haar packets and reports decoder model bytes separately.
The alarm experiment calibrates on March 2021, scores all of April 2021
before labels are read for reporting, and evaluates every one of nine
March-derived thresholds for both dense residual energy and Level-A SoD
event count.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it specifies a reproducible
evaluation protocol that prevents path pseudoreplication, uncharged payload,
test-set operating-point selection, and label leakage. Second, it reports
the complete all-capacity codec result: bounded SoD does not lead any tested
capacity for either D04 or D24. Third, it reports the complete cold-start
alarm grid without selecting a favourable threshold; the observed
false-call, delay, and coverage outcomes do not support an operational alarm
claim. The OGW resource and the long-term guided-wave dataset are public
data sources documented by Moll et al.\ \cite{moll2019ogw} and Yang et
al.\ \cite{yang2025dataset}, respectively.
